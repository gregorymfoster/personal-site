# Reflections on podcast episode

Recently the Rippling CPO’s podcast episode was passed around my team at work. It both resonated with folk’s feelings, and always was spicy enough to make us think. I wanted to write down various takeaways that struck me, for further reflection and to possibly incorporate into my mental models.

Rough quote: “incredible results demand incredible effort”

Intensity drops off in concentric circles

Listening to Matt, you immediately hear that he’s a wickedly intense man - or at least communicates himself to be. He makes an argument that companies need the central leader / founder CEO to be the most intense, urgent, and obsessed person in the business. Each layer of management under the center node experiences a falloff of intensity, urgency, and obsession. The founder CEO will work 7 days a week, hound, and die if they’re not successful. Their reporting managers will push very hard, work late nights weekdays, and do their best to be successful. Those manager’s reports will work hard, do their best while living balanced lives, and would generally like the company to be successful.

You see? Each layer drops off a notch in urgency and intensity. Why? For one, the founder ceo has perfect incentives (usually). They’re root responsible for costs, failures, and also most of the upside of success. Middle managers have some large skin in the game, but less - and aren’t responsible for all failures. By the time you get to ICs, you start finding people who both want to do a good job intrinsically, but also who’d like to be extractive to the company and who are very unresponsive to any large failures.

Second reason for this dropoff - it’s uncomfortable holding others to an intense bar. The managers need to set the constants for others, and challenge them to be uncomfortable. Parents do this, sports coaches do this, and managers do this. It’s uncomfortable making other people uncomfortable - unless you’re a sociopath. So, in reality, many managers do a partial job. When asked for their org to move mountains, they pass the message down, but dilute it with some empathy and care. But they dilute it none the less. It’s human, but results in compounding falloff.

What’s the winning move? Every member of a company should perfectly mirror the intensity, urgency, and obsession of their leader downwards. (Even better if you can uplevel it, but at the very least mirror it). If you are a leader and manager, there’s almost no upper bound in how die hard memishly passionate and obsessive you can be. Not because it itself is maximally efficient and healthy, but because you know you’re role-modeling the bar that others will follow with some dilution. Accounting for the dilution, you need to go overboard as a leader.
￼
Key to this approach is a few foundational understandings. Matt calls this out in the podcast. In a vacuum, it’s easy to hear this philosophy and mark it up as grind culture bullshit. How can you sleep at night knowing you’re pushing so many other people into a zone of discomfort? Why are you maximizing shareholder value so hard?

The reality is everyone in tech is paid plenty well enough. To lead into a zone of discomfort, you have to do it for the love of the game. Excellent management approaches that of a sports game - it doesn’t matter when your soul is judged in heaven or when your parents are on their deathbed - but it certainly is a fun craft and sport worthy of attempting to be the best in. And with the right mindset, people want to be pushed. They want to work hard, and do amazing things. Players on a sport team are constantly uncomfortable and out of breath. But they love it - they’re passionate and want to flex their abilities and see what’s possible. Intensity and obsession isn’t a bad thing to push onto the people you lead - one needs to view it as a positive and beautiful thing.

Key in this are some basic assumptions. Free will is one - folks of course should always have the option to opt out, quit, find a new job. Luckily this is always true in tech and software engineering - there’s not one person who’s quit or been fired at my company who hasn’t immediately found great new work. Everyone is paid well, and no one is going to the street.

Another key assumption is, like sports, no one should be challenged to the point that it’s critically unhealthy. It’s a game, and not worth pushing people into depression, self harm, or missing their family member’s wedding. Play the game maximally, but remember it’s still less important than key aspects of life.

I find these ideas together quite freeing. High performing teams want to be pushed. It’s all a game and doesn’t really matter. And therefore, why not adopt as a leader a maximally intense, obsessed, urgent mindset? Try your hardest, and challenge everyone around you to try their hardest. Act with kindness, speak with empathy, and yet still be demanding of greatness.

You can do all of this without being overly harsh, crass, or mean. You can still be kind, charismatic, and friendly. In my head, I imagine the best sports coaches

During the podcast, Matt hints at a subtle but critical detail. It’s much easier to demand full dedication from a team when things are going well, then when they’re not. I created Graphite, and now work at Cursor. There were points at Graphite where we challenged engineering teams to work weekends and code their best quality work, all while very few users were adopting the product. We had failed launches, and early pivots. It’s like being a member of a struggling sports team - why train unbelievably hard when the results just aren’t there? On the other hand, taking the same team and joining cursor, it’s obvious how much more rewarding it is to work maximally intensely. The work matters. The code ships to literally millions. The outcome might be the best work of everyone’s careers - if only they try their hardest. Obviously it’s easier to be intense, obsessive, and urgent when things are going well. What’s the result from this observation? Play the sport of tech and business. Be the best and die hard. But also work to join the winning team. Be willing to pivot or quit if it’ll get you onto a more impactful piece of work - it sucks grinding for something that’s not working. But that doesn’t mean you shouldn’t grind, it just means you should focus the grinding towards getting to something that’s works.

Another case where demanding incredible input falls apart is when the team/ reports don’t agree with the direction. 1) as I said above, opportunity for impact needs to be there. But 2), folks need to agree with the direction you’re sailing in. If you demand the team builds a billing system they think is destined to fail, or you demand they sprint on a project that will surely get killed later, folks will struggle to accept your demanding push. As a prequesisit, the reports need to really agree with the strategy and plan if they’re really to give their all into it. As a leader you still need to build that buyin and get folks to follow the logic. Otherwise folk will just quit or drag their feet.
